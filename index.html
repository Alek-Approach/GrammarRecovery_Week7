Study box A Relative clauses: defining vs. non-defining

Defining or non-defining?

DefiningNon-defining
JobTells us which one. Without it, the sentence is unclear.Adds extra information. Without it, the sentence still works.
CommasNo commasCommas (or dashes) around the clause
that?Yes, for people and thingsNever
Leave out the pronoun?Yes, when it is the object of the clauseNever
ExampleThe report (that) you sent me was excellent.Our CEO, who joined in 2020, is retiring.

Choosing the pronoun

WordUseExample
whopeopleThe nurse who helped me was very kind.
whichthings; also a whole earlier clauseHe arrived late, which annoyed everyone.
thatpeople or things, defining clauses onlyThe phone that I ordered hasn't arrived.
whosepossession (people or things)Students whose essays are late lose points.
where / whenplaces / timesthe town where I grew up · the year when we met
whomformal object, especially after a prepositionthe person to whom I spoke · many of whom

Watch out: no extra pronoun (the book that I bought it) · no that after a preposition (the person to that I spoke) · prepositions can go at the end in normal English: the person I spoke to.

Study box B Reduced relatives & participle clauses

Reduced relative clauses

When the relative pronoun is the subject, you can often cut the pronoun and be:

FormMeaningFull clause → reduced
-ingactivePeople who live living near the airport complain about noise.
past participlepassiveCars that are made made in Mexico are often cheaper.

Don't reduce when the pronoun is the object (the man I met is already short), or for one finished past action: the man calling you yesterday → the man who called you yesterday.

Adverbial participle clauses

FormMeaningExample
-ingsame time / reasonWalking to work, I saw an accident.
Having + past participleearlier actionHaving finished the report, she went home.
Not + -ingnegative reasonNot knowing the answer, I stayed quiet.
past participle / Having beenpassiveBorn in Lima, he speaks Spanish. · Having been warned, we left early.

The golden rule: the subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause. Walking into the room, the lights went off. (The lights didn't walk!) → Walking into the room, I saw the lights go off.

Study box C Noun clauses as subjects and objects

What is a noun clause?

A clause that does the job of a noun: it can be the object or the subject of a verb.

TypeAs objectAs subject
that-clause (statements)I think (that) the plan will work.That he resigned shocked us. formal
= It shocked us that he resigned.
wh-clauseNobody knows why he left.What attracts them is simple. (article, Act. 2)
if / whether (yes/no)I'm not sure if / whether she agreed.Whether small cities can grow… is the question. (article)
the fact thatI'm worried about the fact that rents are rising.The fact that rents are rising worries residents.

Statement word order inside the clause: Can you tell me where is the station? → Can you tell me where the station is? · No do/does/did: I don't know what does she want → I don't know what she wants. · Only whether (not if) can begin a subject clause.

Study box D Run-ons, comma splices & fragments

Three sentence-boundary problems

ProblemWhat it isExample ✗
Run-ontwo complete sentences with no punctuation between themThe deadline is Friday we need to start now.
Comma splicetwo complete sentences joined only by a commaThe deadline is Friday, we need to start now.
Fragmentan incomplete sentence: no subject, no main verb, or a lonely dependent clauseBecause the deadline is Friday.

Five ways to repair a run-on or comma splice

ToolRepaired sentence
1. PeriodThe deadline is Friday. We need to start now.
2. SemicolonThe deadline is Friday; we need to start now.
3. Comma + and, but, or, so, yet, for, norThe deadline is Friday, so we need to start now.
4. Subordinator (because, although, since, when…)Because the deadline is Friday, we need to start now.
5. Semicolon or period + transitionThe deadline is Friday; therefore, we need to start now.

Watch out: however, therefore, and moreover are not conjunctions. It was cheap, however, it broke. → It was cheap; however, it broke. · Fix a fragment by joining it to the sentence next to it or by adding the missing subject or verb.
